# Session review 2026-11-us-routes

The first session review that adds routes ([ADR-0032, amendment 2026-11-25](../../../../docs/adr/0032-session-reviews-activate-through-a-reviewed-pull-request.md#amendment-2026-11-25-new-routes), [runbook](../../../../docs/runbooks/session-rule-review.md)). The United States held one route, `us-eb5`. This review writes three new ones, each version 1, from 2024-04-01 (the USCIS fee rule, 89 FR 6194, which set the Form I-140, Form I-129 and Asylum Program fees they state), on the `us` official-domain list `us-eb5` already pins. Every source was read on 2026-11-25, São Paulo date: 8 U.S.C. 1101, 1153 and 1184 on govinfo (the 2024 edition; the 2025 edition has the same text in every cited provision), and 8 CFR 204.5, 214.2, 106.1, 106.2 and 22 CFR 22.1 on the eCFR, current to 2026-11-17 and 2026-11-20. The Federal Register was searched for rules since 2024 on these classifications and fees.

## The routes

- **`us-eb2-niw`, EB-2 national interest waiver.** Permanent residence for a member of the professions with an advanced degree (or a bachelor's and five years of progressive experience after it), or someone of exceptional ability, when waiving the job offer and the labor certification is in the national interest (8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(2); 8 CFR 204.5(k)).
- **`us-l1a`, L-1A intracompany manager or executive.** A temporary stay for someone employed abroad for a continuous year in the last three by a company that sends them to its U.S. parent, branch, affiliate or subsidiary as a manager or executive, including to open a new U.S. office, whose first petition lasts up to a year (8 U.S.C. 1101(a)(15)(L), 1184(c)(2); 8 CFR 214.2(l)).
- **`us-eb1c`, EB-1C multinational manager or executive.** Permanent residence on the U.S. employer's petition, with no labor certification, for a manager or executive of a related company abroad; the U.S. employer must have been doing business for a year, so a new office opened on an L-1A files after its first year (8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(1)(C); 8 CFR 204.5(j)).

Each rests wholly on facts the case does not record (degrees beyond an education level, experience, a job's duties, how two companies are related), so each has assessments and no requirement and reads "needs assessment" for everyone. The coverage rule now admits such a version ([spec 0002, R8](../../../../docs/specs/0002-rules-engine-and-knowledge-base.md)). Dependents follow the Act's definitions: the spouse and unmarried children under 21, with adopted children and stepchildren as 8 U.S.C. 1101(b)(1) admits them, for the two immigrant routes as `us-eb5` v9 models them (8 U.S.C. 1153(d)), and for the L-1A as the L-2 spouse and unmarried minor children (8 CFR 214.2(l)(7)(ii)). Costs: the petition fees, the Asylum Program Fee, for the L-1A the fraud fee and, as possible, the 9-11 fee of large H-1B and L employers, and the visa fees; for the immigrant routes the visa and USCIS immigrant fees when the family lives outside the U.S., and the Form I-485 as possible when it lives in the U.S.

## Rounds

Two adversarial reviewers read every item against the downloaded official text and searched the Federal Register and the eCFR's history for changes. Round 1 found no wrong amount and no false statement, and eight omissions or loose titles, all fixed: the adjustment of status fee when the family is in the U.S.; the payer of the fraud fee under a blanket L petition; the capacity of the L-1A's year abroad and that U.S. time does not count toward it; the H time that counts toward the seven years and the year abroad that restarts them; the EB-2's members of the professions, post-bachelor's experience and doctorate rule, and comparable evidence of exceptional ability; the 9-11 fee (8 CFR 106.2(c)(9), revised by 91 FR 51360 on 2026-09-09); and the small employer's definition, which covers an individual. Round 2 read the changed items and found three more, all fixed: the L-1A petition and Asylum Program fees are possible, not certain, since a transferee under a blanket petition files no Form I-129 (a new office must file one, 8 CFR 214.2(l)(4)(i)); a family living in the U.S. without a status that allows adjusting gets its visas at a consulate, so the consular fees are possible there; and the EB-2 waiver's Asylum Program Fee no longer says a self-petitioner may be a small employer. Round 3 read those fixes and found two more, both fixed: the dependents' titles of the immigrant routes no longer say the age is counted as 8 U.S.C. 1153(h) counts it, since the condition tests today's age (leftover 1), and the L-1A petition fee now quotes 8 CFR 214.2(l)(4)(i)(B) and (l)(5)(ii)(A) for the blanket petition. The repository's reviewers then had the coverage relaxation limited to a version with at least one assessment, and the new route's lists read as the app reads them, approved ones only. The quote check, the app's own (`ARCA_REVIEW_QUOTES=2026-11-us-routes`, in `apps/api`), passed in the session after each round. The `review-quotes` job runs it again on the pull request.

## Leftover

1. **Children's age.** The two immigrant routes test the age today; 8 U.S.C. 1153(h) counts it on the day a visa number becomes available, less the days the petition was pending. A child of 19 or 20 can age out during a backlog, and a child over 21 whom 1153(h) still counts as under 21 reads as excluded (as in `us-eb5` v9).
2. **Visa availability.** EB-2 numbers for the rest of the world have a backlog in the Visa Bulletin, which is on `travel.state.gov`, a site that refuses this session. No duration models the wait.
3. **Fees the case cannot pick.** A small employer pays half the L petition fee and USD 300 of Asylum Program Fee; whether a self-petitioner of the EB-2 waiver counts as one could not be confirmed on `uscis.gov`, which refuses this session, so the version keeps USD 600 and says so. For a family living in the U.S., the consular fees and the Form I-485 are both possible, so the possible extra counts both although only one applies. The USD 50 online filing discount (8 CFR 106.1(g)), optional premium processing (8 CFR 106.4) and, for L-2 family members already in the U.S., the Form I-539 are not costs here.
4. **Fees not readable or not in force.** The reciprocity issuance fee for an L visa (22 CFR 22.1, item 23) depends on State's tables for Brazil, which could not be read. The visa integrity fee of Pub. L. 119-21, sec. 100007 (8 U.S.C. 1806) was not implemented by any Federal Register document found through 2026-11-25.
5. **Self-petition by an advanced-degree holder.** 8 CFR 204.5(k)(1) lets an applicant who claims exceptional ability and the waiver file the I-140 for themselves; the version's title says so and no more, since the agency's wider practice is on `uscis.gov`.
6. **The national interest test.** The statute leaves it to the agency's judgment; the precedent that frames it is not on a host of the `us` list, so the assessment states the statute and the regulation only.
7. **Not modeled.** Inadmissibility, including the public charge rule (91 FR 45324, effective 2026-09-18), the State Department's 2026 pauses on immigrant visas (see `us-eb5`'s review), the Gold Card's gift as evidence for the waiver (Executive Order 14351), and two proposed rules not in force (91 FR 57807 on the 60-day grace period, 91 FR 65119 on deference to earlier approvals).
8. **Case facts to come.** Each assessment must cite at least one case fact, and the case records none of these factors, so each cites the nearest it holds (education, income, residence, liquid assets) as context, never as proof. Another thread is adding case facts for a profession, an employer relation, a managerial role and business ownership. Once they exist, a later version of these routes can turn some assessments into requirements.
9. **Production rows were not read.** This session has no database credentials; the proof of publishing is the production build, which runs the migration and fails if the database already holds one of these routes.
